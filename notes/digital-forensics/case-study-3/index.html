01

Executive Summary

This report presents findings from the investigation of an employee after security personnel discovered a USB drive in the employee’s desk. The employee claimed the drive was used for personal documents and that no copy of confidential information was found at the workstation.

The investigation examined forensic images, the USB drive, Windows Registry artifacts, LNK files, Jump Lists, recent file records, and file hashes. The available artifacts support a connection of USB serial A55321 to the workstation, contemporaneous access to confidential materials, and cryptographic matches between corporate documents and files recovered from the drive. They do not by themselves establish which person used the workstation or what intent accompanied the transfer.

03

Technical Analysis of Windows Artifacts

The employee’s workstation was examined with established forensic tools and procedures. The analysis focused on removable-storage tracking, shortcut and Jump List evidence of file access, and cryptographic comparison of recovered files against corporate originals.

Removable storage
Investigators parsed the USBSTOR Registry key, which can contain identifying information such as the name of a connected USB device and its serial number. Hardware configuration data is tracked in the system Registry hive, while user file interaction is tracked from NTUSER.dat. Windows artifacts and timestamps also help determine approximately when the device was connected and disconnected, placing its use within the overall timeline. (Microsoft, 2023)
LNK files & Jump Lists
To evaluate whether data left the workstation, investigators parsed LNK files and Jump Lists. LNK files can record when a user accessed a file, MAC timestamps, and the exact file path. Jump Lists can provide file paths and last-accessed timestamps, helping connect access to confidential documents to the period when the USB was connected. (Cyber Triage, n.d.)
Cryptographic hashes
Investigators hashed documents on the corporate server and compared those values with hashes of files recovered from the USB drive. Matching hashes provide strong evidence that the corporate file and the USB copy contain the same data. NIST notes that cryptographic hashes can identify files and that changing a file produces a different hash value. (Nelson, Phillips, & Steuart, 2019; NIST, 2022)

04

Evidence Interpretation & Limitations

Based on the evidence provided, USB serial A55321 was used on the workstation for about 14 minutes. The drive connected at 2:14 PM, the confidential folder was accessed at 2:17 PM, and the USB disconnected at 2:28 PM. During that window, confidential materials were accessed and files matching corporate hashes appeared on the removable media. Those artifacts support the conclusion that corporate files were present on the USB during this period.

Those findings remain subject to important constraints. Artifact context can associate activity with an account and workstation, but it cannot by itself identify the person at the keyboard. Another employee could have used the suspected employee’s workstation. Investigators should seek corroborating evidence, such as CCTV footage, before fully attributing the act.

Investigators also cannot determine intent from these artifacts alone. The sensitivity of the files and the user’s purpose remain unknown; the transfer could reflect offline backup of the employee’s own work rather than unauthorized exfiltration. The digital evidence supports that the USB was connected and contained matching corporate data, but additional evidence would be needed to conclusively attribute the act and its intent to the employee.

05

Conclusion

The defensible conclusion is that USB A55321 was connected to the workstation for about fourteen minutes, that confidential materials were accessed during that window, and that files on the drive matched corporate document hashes. The evidence does not establish who physically used the workstation or whether the transfer was intentional misconduct.
